# Content, style references and review

Lessons from the animated videos made so far. Add new ones at the bottom with a date.

## Asking first

- 2026-11-14: Don't assume every video should be a fun story. Some videos are stories and some are explainers. Ask for length, topic and style unless they were already given (defaults: 30 seconds, the Storybook look). Don't add requirements nobody asked for.
- 2026-11-14: Also ask whether to plan the video together first or get straight to work. Planning means a beat list plus a design sheet (main characters and key objects for a story, key elements for an explainer) that gets approved before the video is made.

## Using a style reference

When the request names a style or style guide, or gives an example (a reference video, an image, a motion graphic or an earlier video), limit what you take from it to two things:

- Borrow only the **look and motion**: palette, lighting, materials and surface (toon bands, clay, ink lines, grain), camera movement, pacing feel, transition types.
- Don't copy the reference's structure, shot order, specific elements, subject matter or tone. In earlier videos, copying a reference's star charts, engraved plates, specimen labels and blueprint inserts into a story was rejected (2026-11-03) because they had nothing to do with the video. Put elements like diagrams and labels in only when this video's own topic needs them, which is more likely in an explainer.
- A style guide must be pure style organized by category (color, line and shape, surface, type, motion, transitions, composition). Test each line against an unrelated topic such as "how coffee is roasted"; anything that fails is content, so cut it.
- The default style is Storybook ([default-style.md](default-style.md)). Use another one only when the request names or shows it.

## Does it make sense? (review checklist)

Run this on the 1-fps contact sheets of every finished cut, then fix and re-render. Common problems caught in review: long, weird arms and shots that didn't follow.

- Each shot follows from the one before: same things in the same places, same screen direction, the action continues across the cut.
- No disembodied close-up hands. Show the character doing the action in a medium shot.
- Arms are short and tapered, with a cuff and a mitten hand. No noodle arms; no limbs bending the wrong way.
- Characters actually reach what they use: hand on the handle, mouth on the ball, feet on the ground (no floating or sinking).
- Every pickup and hand-off happens on screen.
- Characters never overlap or pass through each other; nothing clips through walls, trees or props.
- Faces read: eyes visible in the shots that matter.
- Explainers: each step is visible and in the right order, labels sit next to what they name and are readable, nothing on screen is factually wrong.
- Titles and labels are readable against what's behind them.
- Sound lands on its action: steps match feet, the thud matches the landing.
